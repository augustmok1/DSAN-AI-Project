What we are looking for

The introduction left an open question: data and software postings collapsed after 2022, but the timing points at interest rates as much as at AI. Before any model tries to separate those stories, I want to see the evidence with my own eyes. So I start with the postings themselves, then bring in the signals one at a time and see whether any of them move with postings, or ahead of them. This page is about looking, not proving. Every relationship here is a lead for the models on the next page, not a conclusion.

Boom and bust

The three series move in the same direction, but not with the same force. From early 2021 to March 2022, Data Analytics postings more than doubled while all postings rose about 60 percent. The fall was just as lopsided: from their peaks, Data Analytics postings dropped about 71 percent and Software about 73 percent, compared with 37 percent for the market as a whole. At the worst point in June 2023, Data Analytics postings were down 54 percent from a year earlier, while all postings were down 17 percent.

The bottom panel is what this view adds to the chart in the introduction. It shows that the data and software series are not just noisier versions of the overall market; they amplify it, falling about twice as far from their peaks with year-over-year drops about three times as deep. Since mid-2024, the swings have died out and all three series have been nearly flat, with Data Analytics stuck around 60 percent of its pre-pandemic level. Why the decline happened, and why it stopped without a recovery, is what the outside signals below help explore.

Three things that might explain it

The introduction named three kinds of outside signals, and this page looks at each one with monthly data back to 2020: economic conditions through interest rates, financial markets through tech stocks and public interest through Google searches.

Interest rates

The federal funds rate is the Federal Reserve’s main lever on the economy, and it sets the price of borrowing for everyone else. When it rises, growth plans get more expensive, and hiring for new projects is often the first thing firms trim. Tech and data teams, which depend heavily on investment in future growth, should feel that more than most. The pattern is unusually sharp: the rate sat near zero for two years, then rose by more than five points between March 2022 and August 2023, the fastest increase in four decades. Cuts began in September 2024 and brought the rate down to about 3.6 percent by 2026. Against the job-posting series, the timing of the first hike stands out: it came in the same month Data Analytics postings peaked.

Tech stocks

Tech stock prices capture what investors expect from the technology sector, and those expectations shape how freely tech firms hire. The pattern has two acts. In 2022, almost everything fell together as interest rates rose, with QQQ bottoming in October 2022. After ChatGPT, the market split: NVIDIA rose roughly sixteenfold from its December 2020 level and Alphabet more than quadrupled, while C3.ai, an AI software company, fell more than 90 percent. That split matters for this project. “Tech stocks” are not one signal, and the companies most tied to AI moved very differently from the rest.

Public interest in AI

The introduction tracked AI inside job postings, where the share mentioning AI more than tripled after mid-2023. Search interest shows the public side of the same shift: a rough measure of how much attention people are paying to AI, and attention tends to come before adoption. Before November 2022, searches for “Generative AI” and “LLM” barely registered. Both crossed 10 percent of their eventual peak in spring 2023 and kept climbing into 2026. “AI Jobs” followed about a year later, and “AI Agents” only took off in 2025. The order is telling: first people learned the tools existed, then they started looking for work built around them.

The timing also sets up a puzzle. Public interest in AI rose fastest in 2023 to 2026, the same years data and software postings stayed flat near their lows. If AI interest pushes job demand at all, it does not show up as a simple same-month rise.

Lining them up

Job postings and interest rates

On the way down, postings and rates line up closely. Data Analytics postings peaked in March 2022, the month of the first hike, and fell for as long as the Fed kept raising rates. They stopped falling steeply around August 2023, when the hikes ended. That is a much tighter match than anything in the AI signals.

The way back up is a different story. Since the first cut in September 2024, the rate has dropped about 1.5 points, but Data Analytics postings slipped from 69 to 62 and all postings from 112 to 102. Only Software ticked up, from 69 to 73. If borrowing costs were the whole explanation, you might expect cheaper money to bring at least some hiring back. The two series appear to move together during the hiking cycle and apart after it, which suggests rates explain the fall better than they explain the stall. That does not rule rates out, though. At about 3.6 percent they are still far above the near-zero level of 2020 and 2021, cuts take time to reach hiring and firms that over-hired during the boom may still be working that off. AI adoption is one more candidate. Telling these explanations apart is a job for the models, not the pictures.

Job postings and tech stocks

Through 2021, postings and stocks rose together. Then the order of events gets interesting. QQQ peaked in late 2021, a few months before Data Analytics postings peaked in March 2022. QQQ then bottomed in October 2022, while postings kept falling until mid-2023. At both turning points, stocks appear to have moved first, by several months. After that, the two stories split completely: by May 2026, QQQ was at 230 on this scale while Data Analytics postings were at 75.

Measured on the raw levels from December 2020 to May 2026, the two series are actually negatively correlated (about -0.67 for Data Analytics against QQQ). That number is a warning more than a finding. For most of the period one series trends up and the other trends down, and any two trending series will show a strong correlation whether or not they are related. Whether stocks really lead postings, and by how much, is something to test properly on the time-series page, where the lag choice for the ARIMAX models depends on it.

Job postings and job seekers

This is the comparison that hits closest to home for me. Searches for “data analyst jobs” were at 30 on this scale in February 2020. They climbed steadily through the boom, kept climbing through the bust and reached 96 in May 2026. Over the same stretch, Data Analytics postings went from 100 to about 60. The two lines moved together in 2021 and have moved in opposite directions ever since.

I read this carefully. Search interest measures curiosity, not applicants, and a Google search for “data analyst jobs” is not the same thing as Indeed’s Data & Analytics category. What the chart does show is that interest in the field kept growing after postings stopped. That matches what I see as a student looking at this market, but it is my own experience, not something this data proves. That is a supply-side story the forecasting models do not capture directly, and it is worth keeping in mind when reading their results.

What I’m taking into the models

  1. Data and software postings amplify the market. They rose and fell far more than all postings did (about a 71 percent drop from peak, compared with 37 percent), so models built for the overall market may understate how much these series can move.
  2. Interest rates fit the fall, not the stall. Postings peaked the month the Fed started hiking and fell until the hikes ended, but they did not recover once rates were cut. Several explanations fit that stall: rates are still well above their 2021 level, cuts reach hiring with a lag and firms may still be correcting for over-hiring during the boom. AI adoption, which climbed through exactly this period, is one more candidate. The models need to test all of them, not assume AI.
  3. Stocks seem to turn before postings. Tech stocks peaked and bottomed several months before data and software postings did in 2022 and 2023. That is a read by eye from two turning points, but it makes lagged stock prices a reasonable candidate predictor to test.
  4. One unusual period dominates the data. The 2021 to 2023 boom and bust is the largest movement in every series. Models will learn mostly from that episode, which may not resemble what comes next.

From pictures to models

The visual evidence points in a clear direction without settling anything. Data Analytics and Software postings behave differently from the market as a whole. Interest rates line up with their decline, tech stocks appear to move ahead of them and AI interest rose while they stalled. But several of the most striking relationships here come from series that trend in opposite directions, which is exactly where eyeballing goes wrong. The next page tests these leads formally with ARIMA, ARIMAX and SARIMAX models, asking whether interest rates, stock prices and search interest improve forecasts beyond what the job-posting history alone can do.
