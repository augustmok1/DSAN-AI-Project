How AI adoption is reshaping data and software job openings

Gus Mokrzycki

Why this matters

Over the past few years, AI tools have moved from research labs into everyday work. Tools that can write code, summarize documents and analyze data are now built into the software analysts and engineers use daily. That raises a real question about the labor market. Is AI replacing parts of these jobs, reshaping what the jobs involve or creating new kinds of work? The answer probably differs by occupation, and jobs tied to data and software are some of the first places we would expect to see change.

The job postings data already shows that something unusual happened to these roles.

Figure 1 is the starting point for this project. Data Analytics and Software postings rose and fell far more sharply than the market as a whole, and both now sit well below where they were before the pandemic. The timing matters though. The drop started in early 2022, when the Federal Reserve began raising interest rates, not after ChatGPT arrived. So the chart cannot tell us on its own how much of the decline is about AI and how much is about the economy. Separating those two stories is the core of this project.

The postings that remain are also asking for something different. The share of all U.S. job postings that mention AI fell to about 1.7 percent in mid-2023, then more than tripled to about 5.6 percent by May 2026, while Data Analytics postings kept drifting lower (Figure 2). Fewer postings, with more of them asking for AI, is what makes me think these jobs are changing shape and not just shrinking.

Why I care

I am a statistics major planning to pursue a master’s in data science, which means the jobs in that red line are the jobs I am preparing for. When I look at postings for data analyst or data scientist roles, I see terms like machine learning, Python and generative AI sitting next to the statistical skills I have spent years building.

Is the market for these roles growing, shrinking or just changing shape?

This portfolio is my attempt to answer that with data instead of headlines, and to figure out what employers will actually expect from me when I graduate.

What we are investigating

So the question is whether the things we can measure about AI, the economy and the markets actually show up in who is hiring. The project follows three job-opening series: Data Analytics openings, Software openings and overall job openings. Comparing them lets us ask whether data and software roles behave differently from the labor market as a whole, which Figure 1 suggests they do. We pair these series with outside signals that could explain or anticipate those movements:

  • Public interest in AI, such as search activity and the share of job postings that mention AI
  • Financial markets, especially tech-sector stock performance
  • Economic conditions, including interest rates, inflation and other indicators

Research questions

  1. How do Data Analytics and Software job openings differ from overall U.S. job openings in their response to macroeconomic indicators, tech stock prices and public interest signals?
  2. Can traditional time-series and deep learning models use public interest, macroeconomic and tech stock signals to improve forecasts of AI- and data-related job-opening trends?
  3. Can NLP-derived measures from job records and descriptions reveal how AI adoption is reshaping cross-sectoral hiring and occupational skill demand over time?

Our approach

We start by exploring how job openings move alongside the outside indicators, using visualization and regression. We then forecast job-opening trends with traditional time-series models (ARIMA, ARIMAX and SARIMAX) and with deep learning models (RNN, LSTM and GRU), and compare how well each performs on the same data. Finally, we use NLP on job records to measure how hiring shifts across occupations with different levels of AI exposure, and extending that work to full job descriptions is the next step. That last piece brings the project back to the question I started with, since it looks directly at the skills employers are asking for.

TipWhere this ends up

Traditional time-series models forecast these trends more reliably than the deep learning models, and the outside signals mattered more for Data Analytics and Software openings than for openings overall.
